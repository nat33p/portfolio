Fetch Rewards App

From scanning receipts to a first Shop purchase.

Designing onboarding that helps users understand, try and trust a new way to earn.

My role Experience strategy, research synthesis Interaction & interface design, flow documentation
Results 34.88% increase in new Shop purchases

Overview

Fetch users knew one behavior: shop, submit a receipt, earn points. Fetch Shop asked them to start in Fetch before an online purchase, then check out with the retailer.

The challenge was to make that change feel like a simple extension of familiar shopping habits. I designed a new-user experience inside the Shop page combining a short explanation of the model, familiar retailers, a first-purchase Welcome Quest and visible progress through the purchase-and-rewards flow.

The problem

The existing experience asked users to choose a shopping opportunity before they understood how Shop worked. Some thought Shop was a place to redeem points; others couldn't tell it apart from receipt-based earning. A dense mix of retailers, products and point values made learning harder.

The confusion continued after checkout. Users didn't expect points to stay pending, or know where to find them — with a 3–90 day arrival window, expectation-setting was part of the activation problem.

?

Opportunity

How might we help someone who knows Fetch as a post-purchase rewards app confidently make their first Shop purchase, without overwhelming them or overpromising rewards?

This wasn't a redesign of receipt submission or gift-card redemption. The business challenge was the path from viewing Shop to selecting a retailer, visiting it and completing a first confirmed purchase.

Research

I built on UX Research's June 2025 activation study: unmoderated tasks with 16 participants, split between people new to Fetch and existing eReceipt users new to Shop. One audience needed to understand a new earning method; the other needed to understand both Fetch and Shop.

of surveyed engaged non-converters said they needed a better understanding of how Shop worked — a comprehension barrier, not a discovery problem.

We started with A/B testing a new First Purchase Quest

"I really like how there's a bar that fills in. It gives you that feeling when you're playing a game."

An evaluative study with five U.S. participants reported an average motivation of 4.4 out of 5 to complete onboarding for a 4,000-point Welcome Bonus — driven mainly by the bonus, with strong appreciation for the game-like progress indicator. 

This indicator also set a more realistic expectation about when pending points typically arrive.

Meanwhile, we conducted quick and dirty usability testing on additional changes to the onboarding UX.

Onboarding system vs tutorial

I approached onboarding as a set of user and transaction states rather than instructions to dismiss: existing Fetch users, entirely new users, people who visited a retailer without buying, purchases awaiting confirmation, and canceled or returned orders.

The architecture centered on three reusable elements: an educational header, retailer shelves and a stateful Quest card. Users with enough receipt history saw stores they already used; others saw popular retailers.

Make the first decision easier

The entry experience answered four questions: what Shop is, how to earn, where to start and why it's worth trying. The header positioned Shop alongside receipts and offers: start in Fetch, check out with the retailer.

Familiar retailers sat close to the explanation, turning an abstract value proposition into an immediate action.

The Quest paired three steps — "Tap a store," "Order," "Get points" — with a concrete reward.

Explain more when needed; stay present afterward

Tapping the Quest opened "How Shop rewards work," explaining the retailer handoff, eligible purchases and store confirmation — on demand, instead of a mandatory tutorial.

On return, "We're checking with the store" acknowledged the visit without claiming an order. Its conditional wording — "If you made a purchase" — mattered: a two-minute retailer visit signals engagement, not proof of purchase.

The scope tradeoff: I focused changes on the header, Quest component and bottom-sheet pattern rather than rebuilding checkout or promising instant confirmation — clearer status, not a new tracking system.

Results

The new onboarding landing page drove a 34.88% increase in new Shop purchases.

Participants responded to the incentive and progress visualization, including a sense of accomplishment after a simulated purchase. Pending messages and estimated timing were appreciated, though some still found the wait long.
